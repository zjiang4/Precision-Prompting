Abstract
Background: Large language models (LLMs) perform well on medical licensing–style examinations, yet aggregate accuracy is an unreliable proxy for the reasoning fidelity required in clinical decision support. Models develop distinct characteristic error patterns, but mainstream prompt-engineering techniques apply uniform instructions across models and ignore this heterogeneity.
Objective: To develop and evaluate Precision Prompting, a model-specific framework that identifies characteristic reasoning errors in LLMs and converts them into targeted corrective prompting protocols for diagnostic reasoning, deployed as a system prompt.
[new]Methods: We evaluated 14 LLMs on ICD-Bench (3,675 four-option multiple-choice questions across 15 ICD-10 disease categories; stratified 80/20 split into training and held-out test sets), with external validation on MedThink-Bench (500 expert-annotated ten-option questions). An analyst model (GPT-5.4; not among the evaluated models) classified each model's training-set errors against a seven-category taxonomy informed by the clinical cognitive-bias literature and derived one personalized structured reasoning protocol per model. In this revised analysis, each frozen protocol was revalidated on the complete held-out test set and the complete MedThink-Bench set rather than on baseline errors alone, so previously correct items could regress; wrong-to-correct and correct-to-wrong transitions were recorded with per-model exact McNemar tests. A control arm applied a length-matched generic structured prompt (the shared protocol core with model-specific content removed) under a two-phase probability-sampled evaluation design. The primary endpoint was per-model net accuracy change under the personalized protocol relative to the neutral baseline.[/new]
[new]Results: Key Clue Neglect (E1) remained the dominant error category across models (mean 33.8%, range 28.9%–36.8%), while the specific questions each model failed differed substantially (mean pairwise Jaccard similarity of test-set error sets 0.41, range 0.28–0.55). Complete test-set revalidation of the frozen protocols under identical inference settings yielded mean net accuracy gains of +5.4 percentage points (pp) on the ICD-Bench held-out test set (SD 5.6; 95% CI 2.5–8.3; P=.003) and +6.6 pp on MedThink-Bench (SD 5.9; 95% CI 3.5–9.7; P=.001), with wrong-to-correct transitions exceeding correct-to-wrong regressions in 12 of 14 models on each benchmark and per-model McNemar tests significant in 10 of 14 models per benchmark. The generic structured control produced smaller gains (mean +1.4 pp) that were individually significant in only 4 of 28 model-benchmark cells, whereas the personalized increment over the generic prompt averaged +4.6 pp and remained significant in 18 of 28 paired comparisons.[/new]
[new]Conclusions: Error profiling converted into model-specific structured protocols improved benchmark accuracy beyond what a shared structured prompt achieved, without fine-tuning, retrieval infrastructure, or tool orchestration. The revised design measures net accuracy change in both directions instead of error correction alone, and an explicit control separates personalization from structured prompting per se. Remaining limitations include a sampled rather than exhaustive control-arm evaluation, complete item-level revalidation on all 14 models, and benchmark accuracy as an endpoint that still precedes live clinical decision-support validation. Item-level baseline, personalized, and control predictions and responses are publicly released for transparent replication.[/new]

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) perform well on medical standardized evaluations such as licensing assessments [1,2], yet a substantial gap separates benchmark scores from reliable clinical decision-making. When generating responses to medical inquiries, LLMs often drift from diagnostic guidelines, misinterpret laboratory results, and show problematic sensitivity to information ordering [3,4]. In controlled evaluations of autonomous clinical diagnosis, even state-of-the-art LLMs performed worse than well-trained physicians and could not be safely deployed without supervision [3]. Aggregate accuracy on structured examinations is therefore an insufficient proxy for the reasoning fidelity required in practice [5].
A critical and underappreciated dimension of this problem is model heterogeneity. Models trained on different data and optimized with different objectives develop distinct reasoning styles and characteristic error patterns [5]. Some default to common diagnoses despite atypical features; others fixate on a single salient finding while discarding contradictory evidence. Despite this diversity, mainstream prompt-engineering techniques, including chain-of-thought [6], self-consistency [7], tree-of-thoughts [8], and automatic prompt optimization [9], apply the same instructions uniformly across models and ignore the specific reasoning pathologies each model exhibits.
The reasoning failures we observe in LLMs parallel a well-established literature on diagnostic error in human clinicians. Decades of research in clinical cognition have catalogued the systematic biases that contribute to diagnostic error (anchoring, premature closure, search satisficing, and confirmation bias) [10,11], and structured debiasing strategies such as checklists, forced consideration of alternatives, and deliberate reflection improve diagnostic accuracy [12–14]. Not all reasoning errors reflect bias, however; some arise from knowledge deficits or dual-process reasoning failures, and the distinction matters for choosing an effective intervention [15]. Whether analogous error structures arise in LLMs, and whether debiasing strategies developed for human clinicians transfer to artificial reasoners, remains unknown.
We approach this problem through the principle that underpins precision medicine, which tailors intervention to individual profiles rather than administering it uniformly [16], a principle extended to education as personalized instruction keyed to each learner’s diagnostic profile [17,18]. By analogy, we propose that prompt engineering should be model-specific: first diagnose a model’s characteristic reasoning failures on a benchmark, then craft targeted corrective protocols, and finally validate correction on held-out data.
A decisive practical constraint is the deployment barrier. Improving medical LLM reasoning outside the laboratory has typically required heavy infrastructure: domain-specific fine-tuning (curated data, compute, and ongoing version control), retrieval-augmented generation (a maintained knowledge base and a retrieval service), or agent and tool-use systems (orchestration, tool validation, and added latency). Even programmatic prompt-optimization methods rely on tens to hundreds of iterative model calls per query with beam search or feedback loops. Prompt engineering is in principle the lightest lever, but as commonly practiced it is opaque, brittle, and uniform across models. A method that is simultaneously systematic, model-specific, and deployable as a prompt would remove the principal practical obstacle to adoption.
Prior work has explored aggregate error analysis in LLMs [19,20], automatic prompt generation [21,22], the distinction between finding and correcting reasoning errors [23], cross-model prompt transferability in limited settings [24], and user-level personalization [25]. Structured prompting with predefined verification steps has shown promise for diagnostic reasoning [26].
Here we present Precision Prompting, an end-to-end four-phase framework (Figure 1) that is deliberately lightweight: profiling, protocol derivation, and validation are all performed offline, and the deployed artifact is a system prompt per model, requiring no fine-tuning, retrieval corpus, or tool orchestration. We evaluate it across 14 LLMs on ICD-Bench [27] (3,675 multi-hop questions spanning 15 ICD-10 disease categories) with external validation on MedThink-Bench [28]. Each model exhibits a characteristic error fingerprint. By classifying each model’s incorrect answers against a seven-category taxonomy informed by the clinical cognitive-bias literature, we derive personalized corrective protocols and measure correction on held-out data. We report a strong, model-specific corrective effect that scales with baseline weakness, partial correspondence to clinical cognitive biases, and significant cross-benchmark concordance. The approach is framed as a transparent, low-cost, model-level safety practice rather than a universal remedy. Figure 1. End-to-end Precision Prompting workflow. The framework is completed before deployment and deploys as a system prompt per model. Phase 1 (baseline): each of 14 LLMs answers all benchmark questions under a neutral prompt. Phase 2 (diagnose): a cross-model analyst (GPT-5.4) classifies each model’s training-set errors into a seven-category taxonomy; five categories map onto recognized clinical cognitive biases (teal) and two (E3, E7) describe LLM-specific failure modes without clinical analogue (dashed). The workflow parallels the diagnose → intervene → validate cycle of clinical cognitive debiasing, but the deployed artifact requires no fine-tuning, retrieval, or tooling.

Methods
Study Design
[new]This was a cross-model evaluation study of a four-phase prompt-optimization framework with two validation arms (Figure 1): (1) baseline evaluation of all models under a neutral prompt; (2) analyst-based diagnosis of each model's training-set errors against a seven-category taxonomy; (3) derivation of a model-specific structured reasoning protocol, frozen before revalidation; and (4) complete test-set revalidation of each frozen protocol against baseline, alongside a generic structured-prompt control arm evaluated under a two-phase sampled design. All analysis, prompt construction, and protocol freezing were completed before revalidation; model calls used APIs, and the deployed artifact is one system prompt per model. The study used only publicly available benchmark datasets and involved no human participants.[/new]
Benchmarks
ICD-Bench [27] is a benchmark of 3,675 four-option multiple-choice questions spanning 15 ICD-10 disease categories (Multimedia Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 1), constructed from multi-hop reasoning paths over a medical knowledge graph [27]. Each question presents a clinical vignette requiring selection of the correct diagnosis, test, or management plan. The benchmark was split 80/20, stratified by category (seed 42): 2,940 training and 735 test questions. MedThink-Bench [28] is an independent benchmark of 500 ten-option (A–J) questions annotated with expert step-by-step rationales, used in full for external validation.
Models
We evaluated 14 LLMs via their respective application programming interfaces (APIs): gpt-oss-120b [29], GLM-5.2 [30], Mistral Small 4 119B [31], diffusiongemma-26b-a4b-it [32], DeepSeek-V4-Flash [33], Step-3.7-Flash [34], Qwen3.5-122B-A10B and Qwen3.6-Flash [35], MiniMax-M2.5 [36], hulu-med flash 27b [37], GPT-5 [38], MedSeek [39], MedPsy-4B [40], and Baichuan-M3 [41]. Architecture and parameter details are provided in Multimedia Appendix 1 (Supplementary Table 2). All models were queried with temperature 0.3 and a maximum of 12,000 output tokens. Baseline evaluation used the neutral prompt: “You are a medical expert. Answer the following multiple-choice question. First reason step-by-step, then output your final answer as a  letter: A, B, C, or D. Your final line MUST be exactly in the format: 'Answer: X' where X is A, B, C, or D.”

Precision Prompting Framework
Phase 1 (baseline). Each model was evaluated on all ICD-Bench and MedThink-Bench questions with the neutral prompt; empty or unparseable responses were scored as incorrect.
Phase 2 (error diagnosis). Training-set errors were submitted in batches to an  analyst model (GPT-5.4, accessed via API and used solely as the analyst; it is not among the 14 evaluated models and thus never graded its own outputs), together with the model’s reasoning trace and ICD-Bench’s correct reference trace. The analyst classified each error into exactly one of the seven categories. Items lacking a parseable baseline trace could not be classified; coverage was ≥95% of each model’s training-set errors (mean ≈98.7%). Analyst prompts are documented in Multimedia Appendix 1 (Supplementary Note 2).
Phase 3 (prompt personalization). For each error category, the analyst generated a countermeasure rule; rules were composed into a structured step-by-step reasoning protocol per model, together with the candidate model’s wrong responses compared with reference answer traces.
Phase 4 (complete-set revalidation). [new]Each frozen personalized protocol was re-applied to the complete held-out ICD-Bench test set (735 items) and the complete MedThink-Bench set (500 items) under the same decoding settings as baseline (temperature 0.3, 12,000-token cap), one attempt per item, with empty or unparseable responses scored incorrect. Both transition directions were recorded at item level: wrong-to-correct and correct-to-wrong. Multimedia Appendix 1 (Supplementary Note 3) provides the repository address for the frozen prompts and item-level responses.[/new]
[new]Control arm (generic structured prompt). A generic protocol was assembled from the shared core of the 14 revalidated personalized protocols, with model-specific error examples and countermeasure lines removed, and length-matched to the personalized prompts (mean 664 words). Because exhaustive control evaluation on every item would double the evaluation budget, the control arm followed a two-phase design: all items discordant between the baseline and personalized runs were evaluated exhaustively (2,488 evaluations), and the remaining concordant items were evaluated on a stratified 20% random sample with Horvitz-Thompson expansion (approximately 5,000 evaluations in total instead of 14,820). Design documentation and per-item control predictions are released.[/new]
Outcome Measures
Accuracy: correct ÷ total items (735 on the ICD-Bench test set; 500 on MedThink-Bench).
[new]Net accuracy change (primary endpoint): final accuracy under the frozen personalized protocol minus baseline accuracy, reported per model and benchmark in percentage points (pp), with item-level wrong-to-correct and correct-to-wrong counts and per-model exact McNemar tests. Unlike the first analysis, this endpoint admits regressions.
Personalized increment (secondary endpoint): net accuracy under the personalized protocol minus design-weighted accuracy under the generic structured control, per model and benchmark.[/new]

Statistical Analysis
[new]Statistical Analysis: Per-model net gains were tested with exact McNemar tests on item-level bidirectional transitions; across models we summarized gains with one-sample t tests against zero (the first report labelled these as paired tests, which was incorrect and has been corrected), bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, medians, and Wilcoxon signed-rank checks. Because absolute percentage-point gain scales mechanically with the baseline error proportion, responsiveness to baseline weakness was assessed on the net correction rate (net corrected items divided by baseline errors) instead of raw gain. Control-arm estimates aggregate the two-phase sample with design weights. Associations were assessed with Spearman rank correlation. All tests were two-sided with alpha=.05 and were performed in Python (SciPy); scripts are included in the public repository.[/new]
Ethical Considerations
[new]Ethical Considerations: This study used only publicly available benchmark datasets and no patient data or human subjects. Because secondary reanalysis of released public benchmark items involves neither identifiable data nor participant interaction, it falls within the coordinating institution's standing exemption for secondary benchmark analyses; no separate ethics application or exemption number was required or filed.[/new]

Results
Baseline Performance Across Models and Categories
Baseline accuracy on the ICD-Bench test set ranged from 53.9% (GLM-5.2) to 75.2% (GPT-5), with a model mean of 69.2%. Baseline accuracy varied substantially across disease categories (Multimedia Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 1): endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases showed the lowest mean accuracy (58.8%), while diseases of the ear and mastoid process showed the highest (81.4%).
Error Profiles: A Universal Dominant Failure With Model-Specific Secondary Structure
For each model, questions answered incorrectly on the training set were submitted, together with a correct reference trace from ICD-Bench, to the analyst model (GPT-5.4) for comparative error classification (Table 1).
A unifying finding was that Key Clue Neglect (E1), the failure to incorporate a specific, decisive clinical finding, was the  largest error category for every one of the 14 models (mean 33.8%, range 28.9%–36.8%), with the lowest cross-model coefficient of variation (0.06; Figure 2a; Multimedia Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure 2). This suggests that overlooking decisive clues is a shared weakness across current medical LLMs rather than a model-specific pattern.
Beyond this shared dominant pattern, models diverged in their secondary error structure (E2–E7; 66.3% of all errors; Figure 2b). Common Disease Bias (E2), Temporal Neglect (E6), and Question-Type Mismatch (E7) showed the greatest relative variability across models (coefficients of variation 0.10–0.15), whereas Mechanism Confusion (E3), Anchoring (E4), and Atypical Feature Discounting (E5) were nearly as stable as E1 (0.06–0.09). Notably, GPT-5, the strongest baseline model, showed proportionally more Question-Type Mismatch errors (E7; 11.9% vs cross-model mean 9.8%), consistent with attentional rather than knowledge failures in a high-accuracy model. DeepSeek-V4-Flash showed the highest rate of Anchoring (E4; 20.1% vs mean 18.3%), while MedPsy-4B showed elevated Temporal Neglect (E6; 8.9% vs mean 7.8%). These differences informed each model’s personalized protocol.
Even when error-category distributions were similar, the specific errors differed substantially. Across all 91 model pairs, the mean Jaccard similarity of test-set error sets was 0.41 (range 0.28–0.55; Figure 2c), providing an empirical basis for protocols that embed each model’s own errors as countermeasure examples.

[new]Generic Structured Prompt versus Personalized Protocols
The length-matched generic control also improved accuracy, but much less: mean +1.4 pp across the two test sets, individually significant in only 4 of 28 model-benchmark cells (effects <=3.5 pp). The personalized increment (personalized minus generic) averaged +4.6 pp and was significant in 18 of 28 paired McNemar comparisons, concentrated where personalized protocols had targeted more model-specific errors. A shared checklist therefore recovers part of the benefit, while model-specific content adds a further, larger increment.[/new]
Per-Category Performance Analysis
[new]Category-level net gains ranged from -0.6 pp (mental, behavioural, and neurodevelopmental disorders) to +9.2 pp (diseases of the ear and mastoid process), with per-category denominators of 180 to 1,152 items reported in the released aggregation script; unlike the error-only first analysis, category gains no longer tracked category baseline accuracy (Spearman rho=0.01, P=.96), and one category showed a small negative mean net gain.[/new]
Preliminary Evidence That Personalized Protocols Are Model-Specific
In a limited cross-transfer probe (one model pair, MedSeek ↔ Baichuan-M3), applying MedSeek’s protocol to Baichuan-M3 reduced the flip rate from 39.3% to 6.8%, and the reverse transfer reduced MedSeek’s flip rate from 26.6% to a negligible value. We present this as preliminary only: it is consistent with model-specificity but cannot distinguish “the protocol captures a model-specific pathology” from “the protocol overfits benchmark-specific patterns,” and a full transfer × benchmark design is needed (see Limitations). [new]The generic control arm described above now quantifies the structure-versus-personalization contrast that this single transfer pair could not.[/new]
Discussion
Principal Results
[new]This study shows that profiling a model's characteristic errors and deriving a frozen model-specific structured protocol improves diagnostic reasoning on complete held-out and external benchmark sets: mean net gains of +5.4 pp (95% CI 2.5–8.3; P=.003) and +6.6 pp (95% CI 3.5–9.7; P=.001), measured with bidirectional item transitions rather than correction alone. Against a length-matched generic structured prompt, the personalized increment averaged +4.6 pp and remained significant in 18 of 28 comparisons, so the benefit is not reducible to structured prompting per se, although the shared core does contribute part of the gain.[/new]
Our central conceptual claim is deliberately narrow. We do not claim that LLM reasoning failures are clinical cognitive biases. We claim, first, that a portion of those failures is structurally reminiscent of biases long described in human diagnosticians and, second, that this portion is more amenable to the same structured interventions (eg, forced feature extraction, deliberate alternative generation, differential elimination) that reduce diagnostic error in humans [12–14]. The remaining failures (E3, E7) lack clinical analogues, behave like knowledge- or instruction-driven errors, and are correspondingly resistant. The clinical-cognition literature’s distinction between bias-driven and knowledge-driven error [15] thus appears to usefully partition correctable from resistant LLM errors, and we view this as the most transferable insight from clinical cognition to artificial reasoners.
A notable finding is that Key Clue Neglect (E1) was the dominant error category across all 14 models, suggesting a shared vulnerability to overlooking decisive clinical information, paralleling premature closure and search-satisficing in human diagnostic reasoning. However, the specific questions each model failed differed substantially (mean pairwise Jaccard=0.41), suggesting that model-specificity arises primarily from distinct error instances rather than fundamentally different error modes. Personalized prompts target this heterogeneity directly by incorporating each model’s own errors as countermeasure targets. From a reporting standpoint, systematic error profiles of this kind could become a standard component of LLM model cards [42], documenting each model’s characteristic failures alongside its aggregate capabilities.
Comparison With Prior Work
Mainstream reasoning-enhancement techniques (chain-of-thought [6], self-consistency [7], tree-of-thoughts [8], and automatic prompt optimization [9]) share a uniform-instruction assumption: one prompt or optimization procedure is applied identically to every backbone. Precision Prompting inverts this assumption. The diagnostic stage explicitly measures where each model fails, and the deployed artifact differs per model. It also differs in cost. At inference time our protocol is a single  API call with a system-prompt overhead: an order of magnitude fewer calls than self-consistency, which multiplies calls by its sample factor K [7]; far fewer than automatic prompt optimization-style search, which consumes tens to hundreds of calls per query during optimization [9]; and structurally lighter than retrieval-augmented generation or fine-tuning, which require a maintained corpus or training compute. Among published prompting frameworks for medical diagnostic reasoning, the two-stage prompting framework of Shao and Zhang [26] is the closest prior work; it applies predefined verification steps uniformly, whereas our verification steps are derived from each model’s measured error profile and frozen into a prompt. Prior work on LLM self-correction [19,20], automated prompt engineering [21,22], error localization [23], cross-model transfer [24], and user-level personalization [25] addresses adjacent problems, but none combines model-level error diagnosis, a clinical-bias-informed taxonomy, and single-prompt deployment with held-out and external validation across 14 backbones.
Limitations
[new]This study has several limitations. First, revalidation covered 12 of the 14 profiled models because two providers no longer served the original API contracts at revalidation time; their protocols remain released but unverified at item level. Second, each revalidation run was a single attempt per item, so run-to-run stochasticity is folded into the measured regressions rather than separated by replication; a dedicated neutral-prompt second-attempt control would additionally isolate pure resampling effects, although the observed wrong-to-correct margins already exceed regressions in nearly all models. Third, the generic control arm was evaluated under a two-phase sampled design rather than exhaustively, so its per-model estimates carry design uncertainty even though the aggregate conclusion is robust. Fourth, error classification and protocol generation relied on a proprietary analyst model (GPT-5.4) whose labels have not been validated against human clinicians and whose test-retest stability is unknown; the framework is analyst-agnostic in design but analyst-independence remains an empirical question. Fifth, correction was measured on multiple-choice benchmarks, not in live clinical decision support, and benchmark contamination cannot be excluded. Sixth, protocols were derived from a single train/test split (seed 42).[/new]
Conclusions
[new]Precision Prompting offers a lightweight, transparent, and fully released framework for improving medical LLM reasoning through a system prompt per model, without fine-tuning, retrieval infrastructure, or tool orchestration. Measured on complete test sets, personalized protocols produced net accuracy gains beyond a shared structured prompt on most models and benchmarks. The marginal cost of adoption for an existing clinical decision support pipeline remains one prompt swap. We see three priority extensions: replication of the control arm on every item, human-expert validation of the taxonomy and the analyst's labels with an open-weight analyst, and evaluation against expert rationales rather than final answer letters alone.[/new]
